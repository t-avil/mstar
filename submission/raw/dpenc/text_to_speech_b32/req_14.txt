The wind off the North Sea had teeth tonight. Elias Thorne adjusted the collar of his oilskin coat, the damp wool already clinging to his neck like a second skin. He’d been the keeper at Blackrock Point for twenty-three years, and the sea’s moods were as familiar as the lines on his own hands. But this was different. The sky was bruised purple and black, the horizon swallowed by a wall of cloud that seemed to press down on the cliffs. The usual rhythm of the light—steady, reassuring—had been replaced by a frantic, stuttering pulse. The great Fresnel lens, housed in its glass lantern room high above the churning waves, was malfunctioning.

Elias climbed the spiral iron stairs, each step groaning under his weight. The air grew colder, thicker, as he ascended. At the top, he wiped condensation from the thick glass with a sleeve, peering out into the storm. Below, the sea wasn’t just angry; it was *alive*, he thought, a monstrous beast thrashing against the rocks. And then he saw it—a shape, impossibly large, breaking the surface near the base of the cliff. Not a wave. Something else. Something that shouldn’t be there.
