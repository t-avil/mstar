Occam's Razor is a problem-solving principle that suggests that among competing hypotheses, the one with the fewest assumptions should be selected. In other words, when multiple explanations are possible for a phenomenon, the simplest one—requiring the fewest assumptions—is usually the best choice.

The principle is named after William of Ockham, a 14th-century English Franciscan friar and philosopher. The Latin phrase often associated with it is "entia non sunt multiplicanda sine necessitate," which translates to "entities should not be multiplied without necessity."

Occam's Razor is not a rule that guarantees truth, but rather a heuristic or guideline used in science, philosophy, and everyday reasoning to help choose between theories or explanations. It encourages avoiding unnecessary complexity and promotes clarity and parsimony.

For example:
- If a car won't start, it's more likely due to a dead battery (simple explanation) than a complex series of mechanical failures (more complex explanation).
- In science, a theory that explains observations with fewer variables or assumptions is generally preferred over one that requires many ad hoc elements.

It's important to note that Occam's Razor doesn't mean the simplest explanation is always correct—it just means it's a good starting point for investigation.<|im_end|>
