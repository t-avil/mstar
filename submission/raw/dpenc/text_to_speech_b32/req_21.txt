Occam's Razor is a problem-solving principle that suggests that among competing hypotheses, the one with the fewest assumptions should be selected. In other words, when multiple explanations are possible for a phenomenon, the simplest one—requiring the fewest assumptions—is usually the best choice.

The principle is named after William of Ockham, a 14th-century English Franciscan friar and philosopher. It is often summarized by the phrase: "Entities should not be multiplied beyond necessity" (in Latin: *Entia non sunt multiplicanda sine necessitate*).

Occam's Razor is not a rule that guarantees truth, but rather a heuristic or guideline used in science, philosophy, and everyday reasoning to help choose between competing theories. It encourages avoiding unnecessary complexity and promotes clarity and parsimony.

For example:
- If a car won't start, it's more likely due to a dead battery than a combination of a dead battery, a faulty alternator, and a miswired ignition system—unless evidence supports the more complex explanation.

While Occam's Razor favors simplicity, it does not mean that the simplest explanation is always correct. It simply suggests that, all else being equal, simpler theories are preferable because they are easier to test, falsify, and understand.
