Deductive and inductive reasoning are two fundamental approaches to logical thinking, differing primarily in the direction of reasoning and the certainty of conclusions.

**1. Deductive Reasoning:**
- **Definition:** Deductive reasoning starts with general premises and moves toward a specific, logically certain conclusion.
- **Structure:** If the premises are true and the logic is valid, the conclusion **must** be true.
- **Example:**
  - Premise 1: All humans are mortal.
  - Premise 2: Socrates is a human.
  - Conclusion: Therefore, Socrates is mortal.
- **Key Feature:** **Certainty.** The conclusion is guaranteed if the premises are true.
- **Used in:** Mathematics, formal logic, and situations where precision and truth preservation are critical.

**2. Inductive Reasoning:**
- **Definition:** Inductive reasoning starts with specific observations and moves toward a general conclusion.
- **Structure:** The conclusion is **probable**, based on the evidence, but not guaranteed.
