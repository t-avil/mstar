Occam's Razor is a problem-solving principle that suggests that among competing hypotheses, the one with the fewest assumptions should be selected. In other words, when multiple explanations are possible for a phenomenon, the simplest one—requiring the fewest assumptions—is usually the best choice.

The principle is named after William of Ockham, a 14th-century English Franciscan friar and philosopher. The original Latin phrase is "entia non sunt multiplicanda sine necessitate," which translates to "entities should not be multiplied without necessity."

Occam's Razor is not a rule that guarantees truth, but rather a heuristic or guideline used in science, philosophy, and everyday reasoning to help avoid unnecessary complexity. It encourages us to favor explanations that are parsimonious and testable.

For example:
- If a car won't start, it's more likely due to a dead battery than a combination of a dead battery, a faulty alternator, and a misaligned timing belt—unless evidence supports the more complex explanation.

While Occam's Razor is a useful tool, it's important to remember that simplicity doesn't always mean correctness. Sometimes, the true explanation is complex, and dismissing it solely because it's complicated would be a mistake.<|im_end|>
