The sea was a bruised purple that night, the kind that swallows sound and light alike. Elias Thorne stood at the base of the lighthouse, his hands pressed flat against the cold, salt-encrusted stone. The wind, a constant companion on this isolated headland, tugged at his oilskin coat and carried the sharp tang of damp wool and distant brine. He didn’t need to look up to know the great Fresnel lens was already spinning its silent, rhythmic vigil above him, casting its beam across the churning blackness. It was his duty, his life, his anchor in this world of relentless waves and indifferent sky.

He’d been the keeper here for twenty-three years, longer than he could remember the last time he’d seen a town, a face that wasn’t his own in the mirror. The cottage, a stone box clinging to the cliff’s edge, held only the essentials: a cot, a table, a stove, and the ever-present logbook detailing the weather, the sea state, and the unwavering rotation of the great light.
