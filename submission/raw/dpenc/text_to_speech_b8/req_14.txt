The sea was a bruised purple when Elias Thorne climbed the spiral stairs for the last time that night. Each step groaned beneath his worn boots, a familiar protest that echoed the ache in his joints. Outside, the wind had risen, whipping the Atlantic into a frenzy of white-capped fury. Rain lashed the thick glass of the lantern room, blurring the beam of the great Fresnel lens into a smeared, pulsing eye that cut through the storm’s veil.

Elias adjusted the oil lamp at the base of the lens, his fingers stiff with cold. The light had to hold. It always held. For fifty years, he’d tended this beacon on the jagged teeth of Blackrock Point, a solitary sentinel against the endless dark. The keeper’s cottage, perched high above the churning water, was his world: the salt-stained walls, the smell of damp wool and kerosene, the rhythmic thump of the foghorn like a slow, patient heartbeat.
