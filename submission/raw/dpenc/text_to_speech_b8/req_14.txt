The wind off the North Sea had a different quality tonight—thicker, hungrier. Elias Thorne adjusted the collar of his oilskin coat, the damp wool already clinging to his neck. He’d been alone on Blackrock Point for twenty-three years, and still, the sea’s breath sent a shiver through him that had nothing to do with the cold. 

He climbed the spiral stairs of the lighthouse, each step groaning under his weight like an old man’s bones. The great Fresnel lens stood sentinel in the lantern room, its prisms catching the last of the dying light and refracting it into a thousand colors before sending it out into the dark. Elias lit the lamp, the flame catching with a soft *whoosh*. He’d done this ritual every night since the year the storm took his wife and daughter. 

The light swept across the water, a steady, rhythmic pulse. He watched it, as he always did, and wondered if anyone out there—some ship lost in the fog, a fisherman far from shore—could see it. Or if, in some distant port, a child was pointing at the sky and asking their mother what that bright star was.
